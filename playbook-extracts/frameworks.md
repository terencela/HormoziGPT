## 1. $100M Hooks Playbook

**Job of a hook.** Sell the first few seconds: get the right person to think "this is for me" and pay attention. Hormozi's allocation: if you have ten hours for advertising, spend the first eight on hooks. He claims better hooks can 5×, 10×, sometimes 100× how many people pay attention, with nothing else changed. Treat that as his claim, not a forecast.

**Two-part structure.**

1. **Call-out** — triggers the "cocktail party effect" (you hear your name in a noisy room). Verbal or nonverbal. Makes the avatar self-identify.
2. **Condition for value** — the hook itself: if *this person* does *this thing*, they get *this value*. Can be explicit ("If [target] [watches this], [they get X]") or implicit (show the result, then "here's how").

People consume when the cost of consuming looks smaller than the promised benefit. The hook sets that comparison.

**Verbal hook types** (with his examples in brackets, for pattern only):

- Labels: *Local business owners, I have a gift for you*
- Questions: yes/no (*Would you pay $1,000…?*) and open (*Which would you rather be?*)
- Conditionals: if/then scenarios leading to a result, lesson, or command
- Commands: *Read this if you're tired of being broke*
- Statements: *The smartest thing you can do today…* / *How to get ahead of 99% of people*
- Lists or steps: *28 ways to stay poor*
- Narratives: a short scene that starts mid-action
- Exclamations / provocative statements

**Nonverbal hooks.** Sounds and visuals (a dropped tray vs. a knife on a glass). On video, pair verbal and nonverbal.

**Finding hooks (70-20-10).** Borrowed from Google's innovation split:

- **70% core:** reuse proven hooks from your own content or others'. Keep a spreadsheet per platform: name, hook, views, link. Review it before every recording session.
- **20% adjacent:** variations on winners (new call-out, same promise; or same call-out, new promise).
- **10% experimental:** new formats and colder audiences.

**Best-performing hook lists** in the playbook are Hormozi/Leila examples from ads, YouTube, Instagram, email, and Twitter. Do not copy them. Steal the *pattern*, write new lines for Swiss architecture/engineering and KI-for-KMU.

## 2. $100M Goated Ads Playbook

**Why ads "hit a wall."** Usually ad quality and creative volume, not a fully saturated market. One founder was making ~5 ads a month; the fix was assembling 150+ a week.

**Awareness ladder** (adapted from Eugene Schwartz), warmest/smallest to coldest/largest:

1. Most aware — knows you; only needs the deal. Hooks are **offer-driven**.
2. Product-aware — knows the category/product, not sure you are the one. Hooks are **proof-driven**.
3. Solution-aware — wants the result, doesn't know your product. Hooks are **promise-driven**.
4. Problem-aware — feels the pain, doesn't know a solution exists. Hooks are **pain-driven**.
5. Completely unaware — doesn't know they have a problem. Hooks are **curiosity-driven**.

Start with the smallest, highest-converting audience and climb only as creative gets stronger. A no-name product should not lead with a national offer hook.

**Assembly line, not "make an ad."**

- Write ~50 hooks, record 3–5 "meats," record 1–3 CTAs.
- Maths: 50 × 3–5 × 1–3 = **150 to 750 ad variants** from one session.
- Prep time split: **80% hooks, 20% meat, ~0% CTAs**.
- 90% of the work is research, not recording.

**Where to find hooks:** your past winning ads; hooks from your own organic content; other people's ads; other people's high-view content; platform ad libraries last (hard to tell what actually won).

**Five meat formats** (mix and match):

1. Demonstration — live use, unboxing, before/after, high-production hero, service results.
2. Testimonial — UGC, founder to camera, podcast clip, raw phone video, walk-and-talk rant, parade of proof, man-on-the-street, influencer.
3. Education — teach a useful piece, then offer the next step.
4. Story — a specific incident with a turn.
5. Faceless — screens, typography, product-only; useful when the founder shouldn't be the only face.

**CTA formula (tell and show):** what to do; how to do it (tap the button); what they get; optionally what happens next. CTAs come last and must match the ad.

**Cheat sheet steps:** (1) pick awareness level, (2) write hooks for that level, (3) write 3–5 meats, (4) write 1–3 CTAs, (5) repeat to find another winner.

## 3. $100M Lead Nurture Playbook

**One job:** maximize 30-day show rates. "If they do not show, they cannot buy." This is the window after interest and before the sales conversation, not long-term newsletters.

**Why it pays (his illustration):** a 20–40% revenue lift at 20% margins is 2×–3× year-one profit, because no-shows are silent lost sales.

**Four pillars** (from his ALAN / portfolio work; he cites ~20,000 leads/day across companies):

1. **Availability** — he calls this the biggest absolute lever. Days open, hours per day, slots per hour, and how far out people can book. If they can't book when *they* are free, they don't book, or they book a bad slot and ghost. Self-scheduling, fewer steps.
2. **Speed** — time to first contact and time to first appointment. He cites InsideSales.com: 42 seconds vs a 42-hour average; Harvard Business Review on responding within 5 minutes (extra credit: under 60 seconds). Book same day, next day, or the day after. Pull appointments forward. Appointments booked more than ~72 hours out show worse, so prefer the inside-72-hours window.
3. **Personalization** — useful and relevant, in the channel they actually answer. Qualify and score (1–5 or red/yellow/green). Send the best leads to the best closers. Use application data and public context. Incentives to show (he likes A/B tests and gift cards — adapt carefully in Swiss B2B). Proof that matches *their* situation.
4. **Volume** — most teams stop too early (he cites 44% of salespeople stopping after the first attempt, 1.3 attempts average). Reach out 5+ times. Double-dial + text, then a decaying cadence.

**Suggested cadence (his tear sheet):**

- Day 1: double-dial + text, three times.
- Day 2 and the next day: twice.
- Then once a day for 1–4 more days.
- Auto reminders: 24 hours, 12 hours, 3 hours before, with name, date, time, and the number you'll call from.
- Manual reminders from a real phone: night before, morning of, 60 minutes prior.
- Many short messages, not one block.
- Conversation loop: Acknowledge → Compliment → Ask the next question.
- **BAMFAM:** book a meeting from a meeting. Never leave someone in no-man's-land.

**Availability targets:** seven days if you can; 9am–9pm as a goal in his US example (reset for Switzerland); four options per hour / every 15 minutes; automated self-scheduling.

**Execution metrics.** Track by person: show rate, close rate, and **lead-to-close**. He ranks "working the list" as work ethic and closing as skill. Culture lines: volume negates luck; yellows are the new gold; process over heroics.

**Show-rate maths in the book:** throughput example of 25 shows out of 100 leads (25%). Use your own baseline.

## 4. $100M Pricing Playbook (*Instant Profit* plays)

**Core profit illustration** (hypothetical, same volume/price/purchase levers):

- 2× new customers (cut CAC), price and purchases unchanged → about **3.5× profit** in his worked example (because acquisition cost eats margin).
- 2× purchases, customers and price unchanged → about **3.5× profit**.
- 2× price, customers and purchases unchanged → about **6× profit** in the same example, because price lifts revenue and, if volume falls less than proportionally, also drops delivery cost.

Revenue can double in all three cases; profit does not. That is the point of the playbook: price is often the highest-leverage of the three.

**Rules of pricing he follows:**

- Price for lifetime profit, not maximum first-purchase conversion or maximum headcount.
- Higher prices usually mean fewer new customers and more profit, if value holds.
- **Close rate consistently over ~50%** → room to raise.
- **Full / fixed capacity** → room to raise.
- Keep raising until the extra profit no longer offsets lost sales. You will hear more nos; that is not automatically less money.
- Raise value when you raise price, especially for big jumps.
- You raised too far if sales stop, or value complaints show up (he uses NPS as an objective check).
- Different customer types often need different prices.
- Gym anecdote in the book: $99 → $299, lost ~30% of members, revenue more than doubled, costs down ~30%. Illustration only.

**Ten plays** (US-centric; several need a Swiss legal/finance check before use):

1. **Monthly → 28-day billing.** 13 cycles instead of 12 (~8.3% more billings per year) if contracts and consumer law allow. Poor fit for annual Swiss software invoices.
2. **Processing fees and a second payment method.** Card fees are real cost; a cheaper method (e.g. bank transfer / QR-bill) can be the default. Check what is allowed to surcharge in Switzerland.
3. **Sales tax / VAT handling.** In CH, quote **exkl. MwSt.** clearly (KI Unlocked already does). Don't hide tax; don't get this wrong.
4. **Annual price increases.** Build the right to adjust into terms. His tables show example 5% and 12% paths; Buffett/See's story in the Price Raise playbook averages ~10%/year over decades. Your number must be earned.
5. **Annual billing.** Discount for prepay. He says in their companies a **~16%** annual discount is a used figure — not a rule for you. Improves cash and cuts involuntary churn (he cites ~1.2–1.7% monthly involuntary churn on recurring card billing).
6. **Round up.** Charm prices and round numbers ($97/$99/$100 etc.). In CHF, test clean figures (e.g. 2'900 vs 2'870).
7. **Annual renewal fee on top of monthly.** He suggests picking a fee **1–3× the monthly rate**, giving a real "reason why," and getting it initialled in the contract.
8. **Automatic continuity.** After a one-time or term project, default into a maintenance / subscription with a clear opt-out, not a silent trap. Swiss Unfair Competition / AGB rules matter here — get a lawyer to review.
9. **Ultra-high-ticket anchor.** A much higher package makes the mid package feel reasonable and captures the buyers who want the top.
10. **Guarantee and warranty upsells.** A paid, stronger guarantee or warranty for buyers who want less risk.

## 5. $100M Price Raise Playbook

**Rules:**

- Don't grandfather forever and don't sell lifetime access unless fulfilment truly costs nothing (he argues almost no large company does this well).
- Raise at least once a year as a habit, after value has gone up.
- **Test the new price on new customers first**, then roll it to the base.
- If you raise **more than ~50%**, talk to people personally, on top of the letter.
- Pair a raise with a launch inside **90 days** if you can (early-adopter framing).
- Do the break-even maths first: what % of customers can you lose and still make more money?
- Shopify-competitor anecdote in the book ($30 → ~$3,000, lost one of 300) is an extreme story, not a template for Swiss SMEs.

**How to pick the price.** The price that makes the most money (unit margin × volume × retention), not the price that feels comfortable. Model clicks, conversion, churn, and LTV together.

**The RAISE letter** (existing customers):

- **R — Remind** them of value already delivered (specific outcomes, not slogans).
- **A — Address** the price change directly. Don't bury it.
- **I — Invest** in their future: what they get next, what you're building.
- **S — Soften** with a loyalty reward (time at the old price, a bonus, a choice).
- **E — Explain** concerns in advance (why now, what happens if they leave, how to stay).

Then tell incoming new customers the new price with a straight face. The letter is for the people who already trusted you.

## 6. $100M Retention Playbook (Churn Checklist)

**Churn** = share of a *starting* cohort that leaves in a period. New sales in that period do not reduce measured churn.

**"Shaking the tree"** after a price or standard change can spike churn immediately; he treats some of that as flushing bad-fit customers, then judges the new baseline.

**Nine checklist items:**

1. **Activation points.** Define the actions after which people almost don't leave. Get every new customer to those actions fast.
2. **Onboard.** A deliberate first-use sequence to those activation points, not a login and a hope.
3. **Incentivize activation.** Reward the behaviour that predicts retention, not mere signup.
4. **Community linking.** Tie the customer to other people (peers, a champion, your team) so leaving has social cost.
5. **Correct or fire bad customers.** They hurt the room and your team.
6. **Annual payment options.** Prepay reduces monthly "shall I cancel?" moments.
7. **Exit interviews or cancellation videos.** Learn the real reason; sometimes save the account; always improve the product.
8. **Survey twice a year** on everything you provide, so you hear dissatisfaction before the cancel email.
9. **Customer journey map.** Know the months where people usually drop and intervene there.

## 7. $100M Lifetime Value Playbook (The Crazy 8)

**LTGP (lifetime gross profit), his calculation:**

- Step 1: average **gross profit** per transaction (or per period for recurring).
- Step 2: average number of transactions, *or* period churn rate for recurring.
- Step 3:
  - Transactional: `LTGP = gross profit × average transactions` (example in the book: $80 × 4 = $360).
  - Recurring: `LTGP = period gross profit / churn` (example: $2,400 / 5% = $48,000).

Whoever makes a customer more valuable than the competition can afford to spend more to get one. CAC can only fall to zero; LTV has no ceiling in this framing.

**The Crazy 8** (ways to raise LTGP):

1. Increase prices.
2. Decrease costs (delivery, failed work, support).
3. Increase number of purchases (more often, or more terms).
4. Cross-sell something different.
5. Sell more quantity (more seats, more units, more sites).
6. Sell better / higher quality (premium version of the same thing).
7. Downsell fewer / lower quantity (a smaller yes when they would otherwise leave).
8. Downsell lower quality (a cheaper tier that still keeps them in the family).

Use 7 and 8 to catch people who would churn, not to cheapen the core offer for everyone.

## 8. $100M Branding Playbook

**What branding is not** (in his telling): a logo, a colour palette, or "being visible." Those can support a brand; they are not the brand.

**Branding vs advertising.** Advertising is paid attention you can turn on and off. Brand is what people already associate with you when the ad stops. Advertising can *build* brand if the associations are the ones you want.

**His view:** a brand is a set of associations in a specific person's head. People do not switch to a "better" product (his supplement story) if the current brand feels like the tribe they want to belong to.

**Four steps:**

1. Name the ideal customer precisely.
2. List what *they* already like, respect, and want to be associated with.
3. Attach your name, people, and product to those things (places, institutions, aesthetics, standards, peers).
4. Optimise the associations for profit: keep the ones that raise willingness to pay and preference; cut the ones that dilute you.

**One-page checklist** (end of the playbook): who it's for; what they like; current associations (wanted and unwanted); associations to add; associations to cut; where those associations will be built (content, partners, rooms, proof); how you will know it is working (preference, price, inbound).

**For Terence.** Personal brand (terencela.com, LinkedIn, stages) is the distribution engine. Leistera and KI Unlocked should inherit associations he already has (Flughafen Zürich, Swiss delivery, SIA-literate, "a human signs off") and not get blurred into generic "AI agency" or confused with "Leister" the plastics company.

## 9. Closing Playbook (OCR)

**Subtitle:** how to turn nos into sales. Training target is the **Maybe** bucket.

Three buckets after advertising:

- **Yes / qualified and ready** — advertising already closed them; don't fumble the payment.
- **No / unqualified** — wrong person; usually an ads or targeting problem.
- **Maybe / on the fence** — they value it but have reservations. This is where closing skill shows up.

**Blame framework** (what people use to avoid deciding):

1. **Circumstances**
   - Time: "I'm too busy."
   - Money: "Too expensive / I can't afford it."
   - Fit: "Not quite right for me."
2. **Other people**
   - Authority: "I need to ask my partner / partners / IT / the board."
   - Trust: "I've been burned; let me think about it."
3. **Self**
   - Fear: "It works for them, not for me."
   - Avoidance: "Send me something and I'll come back."

**Rule of thumb:** "Too expensive" often means "value feels too low *right now*." Translate and compare life/work with the problem vs after the purchase. For otherwise qualified buyers who are genuinely short of cash, appeal to resourcefulness only if the offer is truly a fit — do not bully a bad-fit Swiss firm into a deal.

**All-purpose closes** (80/20; use on anything that is not yes). Adapted, not quoted at length:

- "What's your main concern? What are you afraid of happening?"
- "If this were exactly right, would you move forward? What's the gap?"
- Zoom out: you want X, this is built to do X; what's actually in the way?
- 1–10 interest scale; "what would make it a 10?"; handle that one thing.
- The reason they're hesitating is sometimes the reason to start (especially time).
- Guarantee closes only if you truly offer the guarantee: both options can feel risky, but only buying lets them *find out* with a defined reverse.

**Tone warning for Switzerland.** Many of the gym-style hypotheticals in the playbook (and the "cashiers" rant) are the wrong register for Inhaber and Geschäftsleitung of Planungsbüros. Keep the diagnosis, drop the theatrics, never trap someone, and never invent proof in the close.

## 10. Proof Checklist (OCR)

**Core line:** everyone can make the same promise; no one can have the same proof.

**How he makes proof more compelling** — a hierarchy, not a vibe:

- In-person > virtual
- Live > recorded
- Raw > processed
- Show > tell
- Other people saying it > you saying it
- Identical to them > unlike them
- Personal > generic
- Big results > small results > no results
- Newer proof > older proof
- More proof > less proof
- Third-party verification > no verification
- Proof with numbers > proof without numbers
- Metaphors > jargon (for outsiders; specialists still want precise terms)

**Operating habit:** set up testimonial collection once (he points to the Marketing Machine playbook) and get fresh proof into the inbox weekly. Recency matters; people sort by newest for a reason.

**Quantity page.** A wall or page of many specific stories can outperform one polished case study. He describes a page with thousands of testimonials and a CTA every few entries. For Leistera/KI Unlocked, start with a handful of *real, permissioned* stories rather than a fake wall.

**Local tip in the book:** print real 5-star reviews and put them where people wait. Online equivalent: a proof page segmented by industry (Treuhand, Architektur, etc.).

**Do not invent customers, logos, or numbers to fill this checklist.**

## 11. $100M Fast Cash Playbook

**What it is.** A limited-time, high-margin offer to your **warmest** audience (customers, engaged leads), about once a quarter, designed to create cash quickly — not a weekly discount habit.

**What to charge (his rule of thumb):** take average order value and **add a zero** (10×); "frisky" is **~50×**. The value and the personal work must jump by as much as the price. This is aggressive and often wrong to apply literally on a first Swiss enterprise software deal. Use the *direction* (a clearly premium, tightly capped offer) and set a price you can defend.

**How many:** cap at a number you will **sell out** — he suggests ~5–10% of the customer base when the base is small; fulfilment caps you when the base is large.

**How to sell:** high ticket → consults, with purchase available from day one so one person can spread calls. Large list / lower ticket → launch-style cart open, first come first served. "Reply IN" works if you already have a relationship.

**Why every ~90 days (five reasons):** clears fence-sitters; raises customer value; signals the company is alive; leaves time to deliver; almost all of the cash is incremental profit if the core business is at least break-even. If a quarter feels too tight, twice a year still works.

**ROI illustration** (gym at $25k/month recurring, 25% margin): four quarterly plays totalling $229k extra cash and a handful of new customers, turning $75k net into $281k in his example, at ≥90% gross margin on the play. **Do not reuse these numbers in Leistera or KI Unlocked marketing.**

**Push-to-consult sequence (7 days or until sold out):**

- 7 days out: "something tomorrow" teaser (he prefers text).
- 6: announcement + 1–3 core components + CTA to book; optional price range if the list is hot or you are not yet strong at sales.
- 5: recap bullets + first bonus.
- 3: sales update (how many sold, how many left) + reminder of good/bad avoided.
- 2: spots left + second bonus.
- 24 hours: best bonus, meant to be worth more than the price tag.
- 12 hours: sold out (and optional "one came back" message).

**Push-to-checkout** is a shorter version of the same tension (primer → announce → cart soon → open → last spots → closed).

**Checklist in brief:** pick the offer; cap the seats; write the bonus stack; load the sequence; brief the team; deliver obsessively; wait ~90 days.

## 12. $100M Scaling Roadmap

The PDF in the repo is a **personalized guide**, not a full treatise on every stage. Stages named in the file:

0. Improvise — researcher; get people to try it for free; nothing is really "the business" yet.
1. Monetize — get paid; improve the thing; tell people about the paid offer; take care of the first customers; learn to ask for money; basic systems.
2. Advertise
3. Stabilize
4. Prioritize
5. Productize
6. Optimize
7. Categorize
8. Specialize
9. Capitalize

**Stage 0 success:** enough people try the free thing and like it. Graduate when that is consistently true.

**Stage 1 success (as far as this PDF goes):** paying customers exist; you can describe the offer clearly; you are no longer only giving it away. Graduate toward Advertise when a repeatable paid motion works.

Leistera, from public evidence, is still in **0–1**. KI Unlocked, with packaged offers and published workshop prices, is further along (monetize / early advertise), but should not skip proof and retention.

## 13. $100M Marketing Machine

**Job:** get a steady flow of ads and stories from work you are already doing, instead of inventing creative from a blank page.

**Engine parts:**

- **Lifecycle ads** — film or write the real stages of a customer (before, during, after).
- **Social media scrape** — mine *your own* and your customers' public posts (with permission when it's their content) for hooks and meats.
- **In-person events** — keynotes, workshops, site visits become clips and proof.
- **Communities and communication scrape** — recurring questions in email, Slack, comments become ads and FAQs.
- **Bonuses and award ads** — third-party signals (press, awards, Forbes lists, stages). Use only real ones.
- **Competitions** — structured contests that create content and leads, if they fit the brand.

**Implementation idea:** one capture habit per week (record the workshop, clip the talk, ask for a two-sentence result, log the question that came up three times).

## Cross-playbook stack (the order that usually pays)

1. Offer and proof (value equation from *$100M Offers* + Proof Checklist) so sales conversations are not empty.
2. Hooks and assembled ads (Hooks, Goated Ads, ACQ Handbook, Marketing Machine) on the warmest channel first — for Terence, that is personal LinkedIn / terencela.com, not cold Meta spend.
3. Nurture and closing (Lead Nurture + Closing) so demos actually happen and Maybes decide.
4. Pricing and LTV (Pricing, Price Raise, Crazy 8, Retention) once there is a repeating buyer.
5. Fast Cash once there is a warm list, about quarterly.
6. Scaling stages so you don't buy ads before a paid offer exists, or productize before anyone will pay.
